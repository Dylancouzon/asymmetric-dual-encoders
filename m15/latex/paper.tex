\subsection{Abstract}\label{abstract}

A dense document index is expensive to build and slow to change, while
the query encoder that searches it can be replaced at will. Query-side
distillation exploits this asymmetry: a small student learns to emit
queries in the space of a large, frozen document encoder, so the stored
vectors stay in place. We build Constella, three compatible query
encoders for Stella, a pretrained 400-million-parameter embedding model:
Stella's own query path, Nano, a 34.5-million-parameter transformer, and
Zero, a token lookup table that runs no transformer at all. Over 15 BEIR
datasets they retain 100\%, 90.5\%, and 81.4\% of exact-search quality
at median query-encoding times of 31.6, 2.25, and 0.044 ms on a laptop
CPU.

Because the cheap students are fit in closed form in minutes, teacher
selection becomes testable. Across 26 public embedding models, a
teacher's own retrieval score was a poor guide to its student's quality
under either of two student recipes. A cheap development screen of the
student itself tracked the public ranking, and the strongest teacher
produced one of the weakest students. The savings also move. Over each
teacher's unchanged index, table queries recover fewer of their exact
neighbors than the teacher's queries at equal graph effort, in all 25
measured spaces on two corpora. Matching the teacher takes a median four
times the graph-search budget (HNSW \texttt{ef}). We report both
findings with their limits, the registered evaluations that preceded
them, and the released models.

\subsection{1. The index stays; the query encoder
changes}\label{the-index-stays-the-query-encoder-changes}

In a dense retrieval system the document index is the asset with
inertia. Encoding the 23.7 million documents of the BEIR-15 collection
with Stella took a measured 39.9 hours on one RTX 3080, and a production
index also carries its graph, its quantization, and every downstream
consumer of its vectors. The query encoder has no such inertia. It runs
once per request, it can be swapped between requests, and nothing stored
depends on it, provided its output lands in the document space.

\textbf{Query-side distillation} makes that proviso hold. A student
encoder is trained to reproduce the teacher's query vectors, so it can
search the teacher's document vectors directly. QED, EmbedDistill, and
LEAF train transformer students this way {[}QED, EmbedDistill, LEAF{]}.
pyNIFE fits a static token table to a frozen teacher and proposes
switching between static and contextual paths {[}pyNIFE{]}.
LightRetriever trains a lookup query side jointly with its document
tower {[}LightRetriever{]}. Static sentence embeddings and Model2Vec
supply other table-based representations with their own indexes, and
NanoVDR distills a vision-language retriever into a small text encoder
{[}StaticEmb, Model2Vec, NanoVDR{]}. We claim no priority for the
architecture. What this paper adds is what becomes measurable once the
student is cheap to build.

We built \textbf{Constella} around \textbf{Stella}, the
\texttt{stella\_en\_400M\_v5} embedding model, whose 1024-dimensional
document vectors are fixed throughout {[}Stella{]}. \textbf{Nano} is a
34.5-million-parameter transformer trained to match Stella's query
vectors. \textbf{Zero} is a table of 30,522 learned token vectors; a
query is the normalized, count-weighted sum of its tokens' rows.
Stella's own query encoder remains the third path. All three search the
same populated collections without re-encoding or re-indexing, which we
verified on live Qdrant collections with no rebuild between encoder
batches.

Two findings follow from the cheapness of the students, and the paper is
organized around them.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Finding A, construction.} Fitting a table student takes four
  to seven minutes per teacher on an A100, target encoding included, so
  we could screen 26 teachers under one recipe, then repeat the screen
  with a second student representation. The teacher's own retrieval
  quality was a poor standalone guide to the student's quality under
  either recipe. A cheap development screen of the student was a good
  one.
\item
  \textbf{Finding B, serving.} A student's queries are not the teacher's
  queries, and they sit differently against the document graph. Over the
  unchanged index, table queries need more graph effort to recover their
  exact neighbors, in all 25 measured teacher spaces on the two larger
  corpora.
\end{itemize}

The remaining sections give the setup, the two findings, what the shared
index then allows, and the limits. ``Registered'' marks the project's
pre-registered evaluations, whose methods and decision rules were fixed
before any result was observed. ``Exploratory'' marks analyses added
afterwards. The two screens that extend Finding A and Finding B across
teachers are exploratory: their analyses were written down before
scoring, but they were not part of the registered programme. The
registered evaluations that gated the project, including the ones
Constella did not pass, are in Appendix A.

\subsection{2. The family and the setup}\label{the-family-and-the-setup}

\textbf{Compatibility.} Stella produces normalized 1024-dimensional
vectors. Zero and Nano are trained against that space and emit vectors
of the same width and norm. Zero's rows are exported as int8 and pooled
with square-root count weights, so a repeated token gains influence more
slowly than its count. Nano starts from bge-small, a pretrained small
English encoder, concatenates the mean-pooled outputs of three of its
layers, projects them to 1024 dimensions, and is trained with a
squared-error loss against Stella's query vectors. The prompt,
tokenizer, pooling, normalization, and model revision are part of the
compatibility contract; switching encoders means selecting an available
aligned path, not timed model loading or failover.

\textbf{Quality and cost.} We use \textbf{nDCG@10}, which rewards
relevant documents near the top of the first ten results, macro-averaged
over the 15 BEIR datasets {[}BEIR{]}. Exact search scores every
document, so these numbers isolate encoder quality from
approximate-search error. \textbf{Retention} is the ratio of a student's
macro score to Stella's. Encoding times are medians over 100 real
5-to-12-word queries, batch one, four CPU threads, Apple M5 Pro, three
fresh processes, under a registered protocol.

\subsection{3. Finding A: teacher quality does not rank student
quality}\label{finding-a-teacher-quality-does-not-rank-student-quality}

An existing index fixes the teacher. Before an index exists, a team may
choose which teacher its cheap student will imitate, and the obvious
heuristic is to take the strongest one. Early in the project a table
screen reversed such a choice, so we tested the heuristic at scale.

\textbf{The screen.} One closed-form recipe fits a token table to each
teacher: ridge regression from token counts to the teacher's query
vectors over 337,981 screened queries. The penalty is chosen on two
development forums and frozen before any public dataset is scored. Each
table searches its own teacher's document vectors on six BEIR datasets,
so this is a teacher-plus-index selection study, not a way to move a
student between spaces. Ten teachers were registered; 16 more were added
as an exploratory expansion under the same recipe and selection order.
All 26 share one WordPiece vocabulary.

Under this recipe the strongest registered teacher gives a head of
0.223; the teacher the head's own development screen selects,
bge-base-en-v1.5, gives 0.325. The finding, then: across 26 checkpoints,
teacher score alone ranked these students poorly under either tested
recipe, the preferred teacher depended on the recipe, and each recipe's
own development screen tracked its public ranking. Screen candidates
with the student recipe you intend to use. The one qualifier that
belongs beside the finding is width. Both recipes retain less from wider
teachers (Spearman −0.73 for the head and −0.76 for the table against
teacher dimension). Within one width band, teacher quality does track
head quality (+0.47, +0.67, and +0.36 over nine, ten, and seven teachers
at 384, 768, and 1024 dimensions). Width covaries with model family and
with teacher quality and the bands are small, so this is a hypothesis
about what a cheap student pays for a wider space, not a mechanism.
Stronger-teacher, weaker-student effects are known in distillation
generally {[}Cho and Hariharan 2019, PROD{]}; our contribution is the
consequential reversal, its repetition under a second student
representation, and the screen that avoids it. Registered-ten
correlations, family sensitivity, and the observation that imitation
fidelity does not predict retrieval are in Appendix B.

\textbf{What the screen costs.} Backbone features for the 337,981 fit
queries took 43 seconds on one A100, and each teacher's head grid took
14 seconds; the table recipe took four to seven minutes per teacher
including query encoding. These are the fitting components only. Nano's
final optimization ran 57.3 hours on one A100 for 199,999,721 example
presentations, about \$95 at the recorded rental rate, excluding target
preparation, data generation, recipe search, failed runs, and
evaluation. Zero's retraining was estimated at 20 minutes with prepared
targets, plus 8 to 12 hours to encode targets for a new teacher. Cheap
fitting is what makes the screen affordable; it is not a complete build
budget.

\subsection{4. Finding B: cheap queries change the search
problem}\label{finding-b-cheap-queries-change-the-search-problem}

A graph index is built over the document vectors, and a greedy search
over it succeeds or fails depending on where the query sits relative to
them. Approximate nearest-neighbor search (\textbf{ANN}) over a
Hierarchical Navigable Small World graph (\textbf{HNSW}) explores a
bounded set of candidates, controlled by the parameter \texttt{ef}: a
larger \texttt{ef} is more search effort and usually more of the exact
neighbors found {[}HNSW{]}. Queries that sit off the document
distribution are known to make graph search harder, and graph
constructions exist for that case {[}OOD-DiskANN, RoarGraph{]}. A
student's queries land in the teacher's space, but not where the
teacher's queries land, which is the hypothesis this section tests.

\textbf{In Stella's space.} We swept \texttt{ef} for all three paths
over the same uncompressed Qdrant collections on FiQA (57,638 documents)
and a one-million-passage MS MARCO diagnostic that keeps every judged
positive. The protocol was registered, with exact parity checked against
a NumPy reference. Search time at a fixed \texttt{ef} is similar for all
three paths in these measurements (1.60, 1.53, and 1.53 ms at
\texttt{ef=128} on the million-passage collection). What differs is how
much \texttt{ef} each path needs. To stay within 1\% of its own exact
score, Stella needs \texttt{ef=128}, Nano 256, and Zero 512, with search
medians of 1.53, 2.18, and 3.58 ms. At \texttt{ef=16} the losses are
4.4\%, 7.0\%, and 16.3\%; with binary quantization and no oversampling
they are 6.1\%, 9.2\%, and 19.8\%. Figure 4 shows the full curves.

On one shared binary collection, an exploratory restriction of the
registered sweep, the fastest setting within 1\% of each path's own
exact score is: Zero 0.6116 at 1.11 ms (\texttt{ef=512}, 2x
oversampling); Nano 0.6893 at 2.48 ms (\texttt{ef=256}, 4x); Stella
0.7226 at 21.3 ms (\texttt{ef=128}, 1x). The encoding gap between Zero
and Nano is 63-fold on these queries (51-fold on Table 1's sample);
after search it is 2.2-fold, at different quality levels. Most of that
compression is arithmetic: search adds about a millisecond to both
paths, which is 40 times Zero's encoding time and half of Nano's. The
part specific to the cheap encoder is the extra \texttt{ef}.

Zero's queries also sit differently in the space: median top-1 cosine to
the documents is 0.60 for Zero against 0.74 for Nano and 0.78 for
Stella, and the gap between the first and tenth scores is flattest for
Zero. That is consistent with harder graph navigation, and the next
experiment tests whether it predicts anything.

\textbf{Across 25 teacher spaces (exploratory, analysis pre-specified
before scoring).} Is the extra effort a property of Stella, or of
table-based query substitution? For every teacher in the screen we built
uncompressed HNSW indexes over its own document vectors on FiQA, SCIDOCS
(25,657 documents), and TREC-COVID (171,332 documents), with m=16,
ef\_construct=100, and two builds with different insertion orders. We
then swept \texttt{ef} from 16 to 512 for the teacher's queries, its
table's queries, and its head's queries. Each path is measured against
its own exact top-10, and exact parity was checked on every collection.
One teacher, tas-b, was excluded because its exact top-10 is undefined
at float32 precision on these corpora. The analysis was fixed before
scoring. The teacher's reference is \texttt{ef=64}. The multiplier is
the smallest \texttt{ef} at which the table reaches the teacher's
reference value, divided by 64, averaged over builds, and censored when
a build never reaches it by 512.

The primary, pre-specified measure is the relative-nDCG-loss multiplier.
On FiQA every one of the 25 spaces needs more effort than the teacher:
the median over the 21 spaces that reach the teacher's loss by
\texttt{ef=512} is four times, and four spaces are censored above eight.
On SCIDOCS 17 spaces need more effort and eight do not (median 1.25 over
17 reached, eight censored). On TREC-COVID the result is mixed: 12
spaces above one and 13 at or below, with a median of 0.25 over 17
reached and eight censored. Its teachers lose only 0.2\% of nDCG at
\texttt{ef=64}, so a threshold on relative loss over 50 queries is
sensitive to small differences. That is a plausible explanation, not a
reason to set the result aside. The secondary, pre-specified measure is
exact-neighbor recovery, and it reads consistently. Table queries
recovered fewer of their own exact neighbors at \texttt{ef=64} in all 25
spaces on FiQA (mean −2.9 percentage points, worst −11.8), all 25 on
TREC-COVID (−3.9, worst −18.3), and 17 of 25 on SCIDOCS (−0.8). Matching
the teacher's recovery took a median four times the \texttt{ef} over
reached spaces on FiQA and TREC-COVID, with the tenth and ninetieth
percentiles at two and six, and a median two on SCIDOCS, with one space
censored per workload. The head recipe shows the same recovery deficit
(24 and 23 of 25 spaces). Corpus size is a plausible reason the effect
shrinks on SCIDOCS, not an identified cause, since three datasets
confound size with workload.

The two geometry summaries we measured, top-1 cosine and top-1-to-top-10
margin, showed no consistent association with the recovery penalty
across workloads (Spearman 0.00 on FiQA, +0.23 on SCIDOCS, −0.34 on
TREC-COVID, intervals spanning zero). The penalty recurs; its size is
not yet predictable from these summaries. Four times the \texttt{ef} is
not four times the latency, and nothing here measures a production
request.

\textbf{Query precision is a separate budget (exploratory).} Binary
document codes keep coordinate signs, and the tested engine's default
binary query encoding keeps only the query's signs as well {[}Qdrant
1.19.1{]}. With document codes fixed and no graph, scoring with full
query magnitudes instead of signs raises Zero's coverage of its exact
top-10 from 82.2\% to 93.0\% on FiQA at a 40-candidate budget. Every
path gains, in line with query-aware decoding of binary sketches
{[}QA-Cos{]}. The engine's magnitude-preserving query setting was not
tested here; this is a coverage result, not a latency or relevance
result (Appendix C).

\subsection{5. What the shared index then
allows}\label{what-the-shared-index-then-allows}

Because the three paths query one populated collection, an application
can choose its budget per workload or per device without touching the
index. Two further options were measured (Appendix D). Fusing Zero with
BM25 recovers more quality than any table-side tuning did, raising its
BEIR-15 macro from 0.4572 to 0.4933, at the cost of a sparse index and a
second retrieval, with a sign that varies by workload {[}DBSF{]}.
Per-query routing between Zero and Nano has real headroom: an oracle
gains 0.031 over always-Nano on 12 datasets. The cheap selectors we
tested recover little of it, and a vector blend selected on development
data lost quality outside it, which fits how hard retrieval-strategy
selection remains {[}Arabzadeh et al., QPP{]}. Compatible vectors permit
these policies; they do not justify them by themselves.

\subsection{6. Limits and implications}\label{limits-and-implications}

Finding A rests on two closed-form student recipes over 26 related
checkpoints. A frozen head is a floor for a trained student, the screen
does not select the trained Zero or Nano, and checkpoint intervals do
not cover training variance. Finding B rests on one engine, one HNSW
configuration, and closed-form tables outside Stella's space, where the
trained Zero was not measured. Its three workloads confound corpus size
with domain, and its timing ran on a shared machine, so it is not a
latency claim. Zero and Nano have different training pools, so their
comparison does not isolate architecture, and Stella discloses training
exposure on ArguAna, FiQA, and FEVER. Query intervals condition on the
trained models. The geometry null is exploratory.

What a search engineer can take from this: screen the student you intend
to build. Expect a cheap student to need a larger search budget over the
same graph, and measure encoding plus search together. Treat query
precision as its own knob. The next experiments with the clearest value
are a native, cost-qualified query-precision comparison and a trained
student in a second teacher space.

\subsection{Artifact availability}\label{artifact-availability}

The companion repository holds inference examples, build configurations,
methods with their dated amendments, immutable result receipts,
per-dataset tables, and the full research history including negative
results. The released artifacts are Constella Zero, Constella Nano, and
the Stella document encoder on the Hugging Face Hub. All released paths
run in fp32 through the published loader; a candidate fp16 export passed
CPU parity and failed on CUDA (minimum cosine 0.662), which is why
qualification runs through the actual loader and device. Two inputs are

\subsection{Appendix A. Registered
evaluations}\label{appendix-a.-registered-evaluations}

\textbf{Serving configuration.} Stella is pinned to revision
\texttt{ffeb2b7e}, with its \texttt{s2p\_query} prompt, fp32 encoding,
normalized output, fp16 stored document vectors, and cosine similarity;
maximum sequence length 512. Nano uses bge-small layers 12, 8, and 4
concatenated to 1152 dimensions before projection; batches mix 75\%
queries and 25\% documents. Zero's training pool includes FEVER-train
and HotpotQA; Nano excludes FEVER. MS MARCO is licensed for
non-commercial use, so it was validation-only in our distillation: it
supplied no training targets, negatives, or generation seeds, although
Nano's pretrained base has prior exposure to it.

\textbf{Partitions.} The six-set suite is NFCorpus, SCIDOCS, SciFact,
TREC-COVID, FiQA, and ArguAna. ``Clean-four'' means the first four,
which have no disclosed Stella overlap; it is not a causal contamination
estimate. Four datasets were held out and evaluated once: FEVER,
DBpedia-entity, and the CQADupStack android and english forums. Two
other forums, physics and programmers, select table penalties and
routing settings and were reused during model construction.

\textbf{Reference systems.} bge-small-en-v1.5 and LEAF were the
project's selected targets, not a state-of-the-art frontier. They use
their own document representations. On BEIR-15 they score 0.5171 and
0.5402 against Nano's 0.5081, at 2.57 and 1.39 ms median encoding; BM25
scores 0.4006. Nano is below both on the broad average even though it
passed some of the registered contrasts below.

\subsection{Appendix B. Construction
details}\label{appendix-b.-construction-details}

\textbf{Table recipe.} Ridge regression from WordPiece token counts to
the teacher's query vectors, initialized from the teacher's own token
embeddings, solved by block conjugate gradient with a convergence gate;
penalty grid 1e-4 to 1e-1 with one decade of extension at an edge,
chosen on the two development forums and frozen before six-set scoring.
The fit list was cleaned after an earlier version had 1.31\%
held-out-query overlap. One checkpoint failed the convergence gate and
is excluded. Registered checkpoint exposure is documented; the
exploratory expansion was not individually audited.

\textbf{Head recipe.} Frozen bge-small at the Nano build's pinned
revision; masked-mean-pooled hidden states of layers 12, 8, and 4
concatenated with a bias column; dense ridge with penalty scaled by the
feature Gram trace, same grid, same selection order, same freeze; output
normalized. bge-small-en-v1.5 is also a teacher and shares the backbone,
and gte-small is nearly its linear image (retention 1.008 and 0.978);
correlations without the backbone are +0.09 against the teacher and
+0.46 against the table.

\textbf{Correlation rosters.} Registered ten: Spearman
head-versus-teacher +0.18 (−0.56 to +0.67), head-versus-table +0.24
(−0.49 to +0.74), head development-versus-public +0.71 (+0.12 to +0.96).
Leave-one-family-out values range from +0.44 to +0.60 for
head-versus-table and −0.12 to +0.27 for head-versus-teacher over the
26.

\textbf{Fidelity is not retrieval.} Tables fitted to two e5 checkpoints
match their teachers' query vectors at mean cosine 0.90 and 0.89 against
0.78 for Stella's table, yet retrieve worse; three earlier training
screens reduced their losses without improving ranking. Three
vector-error diagnostics did not rank table quality either.

\textbf{Costs.} Table fitting four to seven minutes per registered
teacher on an A100, inferred from output intervals, including query
encoding except cached Stella targets. Head features 43 seconds for the
fit list; dev grid 14 seconds per teacher; six-set scoring about one
minute per teacher. These exclude downloads, data assembly, and index
construction.

\subsection{Appendix C. Search and precision
controls}\label{appendix-c.-search-and-precision-controls}

\textbf{Stella-space sweep.} Qdrant 1.19.1, HNSW m=16,
ef\_construct=100, indexing threshold 1 KB so no segment is served by a
plain scan, \texttt{ef} in 16 to 512, oversampling 1, 2, and 4 under
compression, exact parity against NumPy on 200 queries per path. Timing
is warmed and sequential with encoding and search measured in separate
phases.

\textbf{Cross-space sweep.} The same engine and graph parameters,
uncompressed, two builds per space and workload differing in a seeded
insertion order, 16 to 512 \texttt{ef}, limit 11 with the self-hit drop,
exact parity checked tie-aware on 200 queries per path. Recovery@10
counts a returned document when its exact score is at or above the exact
tenth neighbor's score within 1e-4, because some tables produce
exact-score ties at rank ten and two exact scorers legitimately break
them differently; the set-overlap version is kept in every row. Four
amendments were recorded during the run, before any space's rows were
analysed: SCIDOCS added as a third workload because TREC-COVID has 50
queries; the tie-aware measure; a 0.995 sanity gate on parity with all
values reported; and the exclusion rule that removed tas-b (SCIDOCS head
parity 0.994). Stella's teacher rows agree with the Stella-space sweep
at every \texttt{ef}. Censoring: on FiQA four spaces never reached the
teacher's relative loss by \texttt{ef=512} in at least one build; on
SCIDOCS eight; on TREC-COVID eight.

\textbf{Native binary pilot and replication.} On one fixed binary
collection per workload with 192 deterministically selected queries,
binary scoring with rescoring at 1x oversampling loses 11.7, 5.1, and
2.3 percentage points of exact-neighbor recovery for Zero, Nano, and
Stella on FiQA at \texttt{ef=64}; the million-passage replication loses
3.9, 3.1, and 2.2, and the extra Zero-versus-Nano interval includes
zero. Native rescoring also changes cross-segment candidate merging.

\textbf{Graph-free precision control.} Same queries, vectors, and
targets; all document sign codes scored exhaustively at global candidate
budgets 10, 40, and 100; expected coverage averages uniform inclusion at
tied boundaries.

\subsection{Appendix D. Fusion and routing over the shared
index}\label{appendix-d.-fusion-and-routing-over-the-shared-index}

\textbf{Lexical fusion.} Fusing Zero with BM25 raises its BEIR-15 macro
score from 0.4572 to 0.4933, 87.9\% of Stella; Nano plus BM25 reaches
0.5110. The sign varies by workload: Zero on HotpotQA rises from 0.6127
to 0.7015, Nano on MS MARCO falls from 0.4063 to 0.3699. On the
million-passage diagnostic a dense-plus-sparse collection takes 4.71 ms
per query including Zero encoding against 1.11 ms for the dense binary
example, in different configurations, not an isolated fusion overhead.
The scores use distribution-based score fusion over 100 candidates per
branch with a bm25s lexical branch; other lexical implementations need
their own comparison because their score distributions enter the
normalization. The operator ordering changes with candidate depth
(Appendix A).

\textbf{Routing and blending (registered).} On 12 datasets, an oracle
choosing Zero or Nano per query with relevance labels scores 0.5473
against 0.5161 for always-Nano, and matches always-Nano by sending the
most beneficial 15\% of queries to Nano. Query length, subwords per
word, and the pooled vector norm are weak selectors (0.002 to 0.005 over
random routing at matched Nano share); Zero's post-search score margin
recovers about a quarter of the oracle gain over random routing on six
datasets and costs a second search for escalated queries. A
development-selected 30\% Zero contribution blended into Nano's vector
lowers Nano's six-set score by 0.0051 (95\% paired interval −0.0092 to
−0.0011).
